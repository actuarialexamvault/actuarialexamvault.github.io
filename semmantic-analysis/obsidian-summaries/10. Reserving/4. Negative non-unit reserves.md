
> [!definition] What is a **negative non-unit reserve**?
> A negative non-unit reserve arises when the value of **future positive cashflows** outweighs the value of the **negative cashflows**. The can occur for unit contracts if future charges are expected to exceed future expenses and other costs.
> 
> If a contract has a negative reserve it means that we a treating the contract as an **asset**. By holding a negative reserve, the company effectively takes **advance credit for future profits** to reduce **initial capital strain** and improve the capital efficiency of the product

> [!out-flow] When will negative non-unit reserves be held?
> In a prudential valuation, several conditions and regulatory constraints must be met before negative non-unit reserves can be held:
> - ***Regulatory permission***: They must be **allowed** by the local supervisory regulator
>
>Other conditions below may be imposed to ensure the company remains solvent and that theoretical future income actually materializes to cover costs.

 > [!floor] ***Surrender value floor***
 > The sum of the unit and non-unit reserve for a policy should not be less than any **guaranteed surrender value**. 
 > - This prevents the insurer from being in a position where it has to suddenly raise capital to pay a surrendering policyholder because it had assumed those funds would only be needed years into the future

> [!receipt] ***Repayment of the "loan"***
> A negative reserve is essentially a **"loan" to other contracts** that hold positive reserves
> - The future profits arising on the policy with the negative non-unit reserve need to emerge in time to repay the negative reserve (which acts as a temporary loan from other contracts)
>
>This addresses the risk of a liquidity mismatch, ensuring the company isn't taking credit for cash it won't actually receive until long after its physical expenses have come due

>[!out-flow] ***No future valuation strain***
> The negative reserve must be calculated such that there are **no future negative cashflows** for that policy. This ensures there is **no future valuation strain**, meaning the company will not be forced to suddenly find more capital at a later date because it took too much credit for profit today. 

> [!limit] ***Aggregate floor limit***
> In aggregate, the sum of all non-unit reserves should not be negative.
> - This ensures the company's total liabilities are still **backed by at least some physical assets** rather than just a collection of theoretical future promises
> - It prevents the company from using **over-capitalizing on new business** (treating contracts with negative reserves as an asset to justify writing more business).

> [!cycle] What is the algorithm for calculating negative non-unit reserves?
> 1. **Project future cashflows:** 
> 	- Project the expected future non-unit cashflows for the policy, income less outgo (expenses and benefits in excess of the unit fund) 
>
>2. **Identify the last cashflow:** 
>	- Locate the last projected cashflow, regardless of whether it is positive or negative
>
>3. **Set the initial reserve:** 
>	- Determine the amount needed to meet that final cashflow at that specific point in time. If the cashflow is positive, the reserve is set as a **negative amount**
>
>4. **Apply the surrender value floor:** 
>	- Check that the **total reserve** (the sum of the unit and non-unit reserves) is not less than the **guaranteed surrender value** (unit reserve minus surrender penalty) 
>
>5. **Iterate backward:** 
>	- Move back to the next previous cashflow (t-1). Discount the current reserve to that point and subtract the cashflow at that time period. Repeat the surrender value floor check (Step 4).
>
>6. **Repeat the process:** 
>	- Repeat these steps working backward through each time period until reaching the **valuation date**.
>
>7. **Establish the final reserve:** 
>	- The result at the valuation date is the required non-unit reserve

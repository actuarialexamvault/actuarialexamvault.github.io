
> [!distribution] What is VaR?
> A risk-based capital measure quantifying the amount needed so assets exceed liabilities at a specific confidence level (usually 99.5%) over a **one-year period**

> [!trending-down] How is VaR calculated?
> - Determine the initial surplus (Total Assets minus Liabilities)
>- Stress test individual risk factors at a specific confidence level, typically 99.5% (a 1-in-200 year event) over a **one-year period**
>- Aggregate separate stresses using a correlation matrix or copulas to account for diversification benefits.
>- Subtract the stressed surplus from the initial surplus to find the "Required Capital"
>- Any surplus remaining after both liabilities and required capital are covered is "[[../../Definitions/Free Assets|Free Capital]]"

> [!trajectories] What are the risk factors that are typically shocked in a VaR approach?
> 1. Mortality
> 2. Lapse/persistency
> 3. Expenses
> 4. Investment performance
> 5. Morbidity
> 6. Operational risk

> [!EXAMPLE] Numerical Example
>- Step 1: Calculate Initial Surplus
>    - Total Assets: 100
>    - Total Liabilities: 80
>    - Initial Surplus: 20 (100 - 80)
>- Step 2: Apply Stress (Shock)
>   - Recalculate values after an adverse event (e.g., equity market fall).
>- Step 3: Calculate Stressed Surplus
>    - Stressed Assets: 85
>   - Stressed Liabilities: 76
 >   - Stressed Surplus: 9 (85 - 76)
>- Step 4: Find Required Capital
>    - Initial Surplus (20) - Stressed Surplus (9) = Required Capital (11)
>- Final Capital Allocation
>    - Available Capital: 20
>    - Required Capital: 11 (buffer for risks)
>    - Free Capital: 9 (excess surplus)

> [!skull] How do insurers select a specific level of the risk drive as a plausible extreme event?
> ![[diagrams/Pasted image 20260302085404.png]]
>- To find the "shock" value, insurers **run** **stochastic models** with thousands of simulations of the risk driver
>- Stochastic models are calibrated to the real world to accurately capture the frequency and severity of extreme outcomes
>- The model identifies the specific point in the **tail of the probability distribution** where only 0.05% of outcomes are worse
>

> [!warning] When would modelling individual risk driver shocks be insufficient?
>- Since VaR aggregates individual risks using **correlation matrices**, it may produce results that are too low to account for a **combination of events** happening at the same time (with a probability level of 1 in 200), which could produce a higher capital requirement than the aggregate VaR. 
>- This is caused by the **non-linearity** and **non-separability** of individual risks.
>- Separate allowance needs to be made in the SCR calculation for these effects.

> [!exponential] What is **non-linearity** of risks
> The capital required to support a risk is not a directly proportional function of the underlying risk driver
> A change in a risk factor (e.g. interest rates) does not result in a constant, predictable change in liabilities.
> - e.g. options and guarantees often have specific biting points, which could cause a massive increase in liabilities.

> [!correlation] What is **non-separability** of risks
> The combined financial impact of multiple events happening at the same time is worse than the sum of their individual impacts.
> 
> e.g. On an annuity portfolio if people live longer (longevity risk) and per-policy expenses increase (expense risk), the company must pay those higher expenses for a much longer period than anticipated.

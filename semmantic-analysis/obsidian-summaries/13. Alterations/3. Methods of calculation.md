
> [!perc] Proportionate paid-up values
> For without-profits endowment assurances, the paid up value is calculated as: $$\text{Original Sum Assured} \times \dfrac{\text{Premiums paid}}{\text{Premiums payable}}$$


> [!question] Does the method meet the principles?
> The proportionate method excels in simplicity but fails on technical accuracy
> 
>|Principle|Met?|Reason|
|---|---|---|
|**Affordability**|No|It often results in values that are too high early on because it **ignores initial expenses**.|
|**Fairness in Profitability**|No|Values tend to be too low during the middle of the term because **investment earnings are not considered** .|
|**Ease of Calculation/Explanation**|Yes|It is a very simple ratio that is straightforward to explain to policyholders .|
|**Avoidance of Lapse and Re-entry**|Partial|Mathematical inaccuracies across the term make it less reliable for preventing these risks .|
|**Stability in Benefits/Premiums**|Partial|It is **less stable** than more modern methods due to its inherent mathematical simplicity .|
|**Consistency with Boundary Conditions**|Partial|While it hits the maturity value correctly, it is usually **inconsistent with surrender values** at other points .|

>[!equal] Equating policy values
> Use for all types of alterations: $$\text{Value before alteration} = \text{Prospective value after alteration}$$
>   
Note: The value of the contract before alteration can be calculated on a prospective or retrospective basis

>[!compare] What are the advantages and disadvantages of the equating of values method?
>**Advantages**
>- **Flexibility**: It can be applied to almost any type of alteration
>- **Accuracy**: it more accurate than the proportionate paid-up value method, because it accounts for the timing of cashflows and the time value of money
>- **Fairness in profitability**: The method allows the company to choose how much profit is released at the time of alteration and how much will emerge of the remaining term
>- **Consistency with boundary conditions**: if the same assumptions and methods are used for both sides of the equation the method produces terms consistent with the company's surrender and paid-up values 
>
>**Disadvantages**
>- **Complexity**: it is more complex to calculated and explain to policyholders
>- **Lapse and re-entry risk**: The method does not always ensure the revised terms are better than those of a new policy, the company must always check this
>- **Basis selection**: The resulting values are highly sensitive to the basis chosen (interest rates and mortality assumptions)
>

> [!EXAMPLE] 
>For a whole life assurance at duration $t$, the equation of value is:
>
>$$(PUSA)_t \cdot A_{x+t} + e \cdot \ddot{a}_{x+t} + f \cdot A_{x+t} = (SV)_t$$
>
>**Components**
>
>- **$(PUSA)_t$**: The new, reduced paid-up sum assured.
>- **$(SV)_t$**: The surrender value (or prospective reserve) at the time of alteration.
>- **$A_{x+t}$**: The present value of 1 payable on death at the attained age.
>- **$e$**: Level annual renewal administration expenses (note that renewal commission is removed since no further premiums are paid).
>- **$\ddot{a}_{x+t}$**: The present value of a life annuity of 1 per annum.
>- **$f$**: The cost of processing the final claim.
>
>If a specific administrative **alteration expense** ($C$) is charged, it is typically subtracted from the value before alteration.

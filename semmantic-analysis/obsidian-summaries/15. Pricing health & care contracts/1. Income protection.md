
> [!trajectories] What are the possible IP states and how can they be subdivided?
>- The basic states are **healthy, sick and death**. 
>- The healthy and sick states can be subdivided by various risk factors and durations to apply more precise transition probabilities.
>- Actuaries must be careful to avoid [[../../Definitions/Spurious accuracy|spurious accuracy]] if the available data for each cells lacks credibility. 

> [!cycle] Define the multi-state model for income protection
> EPV of benefits payable in state $S$ is given by:$$ EPV(Benefit) = S \cdot \int_0^{\infty}v^t {}_tp_x^{HS}dt   $$
> Where ${}_tp_x^{HS}$ is the probability of transition from Healthy to Sick, given by:$${}_tp_x^{HS} = \int_0^t {}_r p_x^{HH} \cdot \mu_{x+r}^{HS} \cdot {}_{t-r}p_{x+r}^{SS}dr$$
> 

> [!compare] What are the advantages vs disadvantages of the multi-state model?
> Advantages:
> - **Deep insight** into how robust a company's rating and reserving structures are.
> - **Superior sensitivity** testing to the simple inception/annuity method
> - More **granular tracking of sub-cohorts**, e.g. the number of previous claims or specific categories of illness.
> 
> Disadvantages:
> - Data scarcity
> - Spurious accuracy
> - Operational complexity


> [!sum] Define the inception / disabled life annuity approach?
 A method of determining the expected cost of benefits from claims incepting in a particular year, commonly used to cost income protection. $$EPV(Benefit) = S \cdot \int_0^\infty v^t \cdot {}_tp_x^{HH} \cdot \mu_{x+t}^{HS} \cdot \overline{a}_{x+t}^{S}dt$$
>
> It consists of the following:
>1. **Survival probability:** the probability that $x$ is alive and well at time $t$. 
> 2. **Claim inception rate:** The probability of falling sick in time interval, derived by multiplying the sickness inception rate by the probability of remaining sick throughout the deferred period 
> 3. **Disabled Life Annuity**: The present value of the annuity then payable on sickness, allowing for benefit escalation, death or recovery and discounting
> 4. **Discount factor**: a discounting factor used to discount the value of the disabled life annuity back to the valuation/inception date of the policy.
> 5. **Annual benefit amount**: The amount of regular income provided by the contract.

> [!compare] What are the advantages/disadvantages of the inception / disability annuity approach?
> Advantages
> - ***Practicality***: it provides a more straightforward and meaningful approach approach for practical use compared to complex multi-state models.
> - ***Avoids spurious accuracy***: It prevents relying on a high degree of detail that the available data may not be credible
> - ***Common usage***: it is the most frequent method used for costing individual IP contracts
>
>Disadvantages
>- ***Difficult to perform sensitivity testing*** 
>- ***Reduced insight*** into the company's rating and reserving robustness
>- ***Oversimplification***: It may ignore detailed sub-classes, such as whether a policyholder has been sick multiple times in the past which can influence future claim probabilities. 

> [!floor] How is the model adjusted for deferred periods?
> To adjust the integral for a deferred period ($d$), we must incorporate the requirement that the policyholder remains continuously sick from the time they fall ill until benefits begin:
> 
> The adjusted integral is: $$EPV(Benefit) = S \cdot \int_0^\infty v^t \cdot {}_tp_x^{HH} \cdot \mu_{x+t} \cdot (v^d \cdot {}_dp_{x+t}^{SS} \cdot \overline{a}_{x+t+d}^{S})dt$$
> 
> **${}_dp_{x+t}^{SS}$** is the probability that a life who falls sick at age $x+t$ remains continuously sick for the duration of the deferred period $d$

> [!sum] How is the integral approximated in practice?
> In practice, the integral for valuing disability benefits is approximated by assuming that policyholders becoming disabled during a year do so, on average, at the midpoint of that year ($x + t + 1/2$).
>
>The continuous integral is replaced by a summation formula: $$\sum_{t=0}^\infty \frac{(ad)_{x+t}^i}{(al)_x} \cdot v^{t+\frac{1}{2}} \cdot \bar{a}_{x+t+\frac{1}{2}}^i$$
>- **$(ad)_{x+t}^i$**: The number of healthy lives becoming sick (inceptions) during the year of age $x+t$ to $x+t+1$.
>- **$(al)_x$**: The initial number of healthy lives at the starting age.
>- **$v^{t+1/2}$**: The discount factor to the middle of the year.
>- **$\bar{a}_{x+t+1/2}^i$**: The value of the disability annuity for a life becoming sick at the mid-year point.

> [!question] What are central claim inception rates?
> A central rate gives the expected number of claim inceptions occurring over the year of age $[x, x+1]$, relative to the average number of numbers alive during that year of age. Given a deferred period $d$, the age at commencement of sickness will be $x-d$. The claim inception rates are denoted by $(ia)_{x,d}$.
> 
> The adjusted summation formula for the Expected Present Value (EPV) of benefits per policy issued at age $x$ is: $$\sum_{t=0}^\infty v^{t+\frac{1}{2}} \cdot {}_{t+\frac{1}{2}}p_x \cdot (ia)_{x+t, d} \cdot \bar{a}_{x+t+\frac{1}{2}, d}^i$$
> 


> [!math] What are the two annuity rates that are used and in what situations are they suitable
> - **Present value of annuities to in claim lives** ($\overline{a}_{x,z}^{\overline{SS}}$)
> - **Present value of annuities for lives currently healthy ($\overline{a}_{x}^{HS(d/all)}$)**
>
>The first bullet point would be used in the claims reserve calculation for **claims in payment**.
>The second would be used in the calculation of premiums and in the gross premium valuation reserve.


> [!steps] What is the first step when developing an investment strategy?
> The first step is to match assets to the **nature, term and currency** of liabilities to minimize investment risk. Start with 
> 1. ***Investment-linked liabilities***: Invest in the exact assets underlying the benefit formula
> 2. ***Index-guaranteed***: Match with index-linked securities or real assets like equities if the specific index isn't available
> 3. ***Fixed money guarantees***: match with government or corporate bonds using discounted mean term (duration)
> 4. ***Discretionary benefits***: Target high growth assets like "blue-chip" equities and property to meet policyholder expectations of real returns.
  
> [!solvency] What is the second step when developing an investment strategy?
>1. Having taken care of liabilities, determine the level of **free assets** available. These act as a cushion, allowing the company to move away from the strict matching described above to **seek higher returns**. 
> 2. Here the company can adopt a slightly **riskier strategy** in its equity selection, and it could invest overseas without currency hedging, property investment is also common usually in the form of its own premises.
> 3. The strategy must be modified to include **sufficient cash** for the company to operate on a day-to-day basis without needing to sell any non-cash assets.

> [!gears] How do actuaries use a model office to determine an investment strategy?
>Actuaries use a **model office** of the business in-force along with a model of the assets to simulate the interactions between a company's assets and liabilities over time, allowing them to test various investment strategies. The process takes on the following steps:
>- Allocate a certain amount of **free assets** to support the assets underlying the
reserves 
> - A **model for the investment portfolio** can be built up based on the proposed investment strategy.
> - **The liabilities** are projected forward on best estimate assumptions, although the company will also want to sensitivity test the variations of the assumptions.
> - **For the assets**, use stochastic models to generate thousands of "sample paths" for variables like interest rates, inflation, and capital values, ensuring all **parameters are realistically consistent**
> - The model generates a **distribution of the** **future solvency position** each year. From this a probability of potential future insolvency can be estimated for a given investment strategy.
> - Check that the excess of these assets over the liabilities exceeds any **minimum capital requirement** for the entire projection period for most (e.g. 99%) of the model runs
> - Calculate some **measure** of **profitability**, e.g. NPV, PVFP
> - Repeat these last these steps assuming different (more or less risky) investment strategies until the target probability of insolvency is achieved; and
> - Identify which of the possible strategies, having **equal insolvency risk**, produce the highest profitability.

>[!caution] What risks or potential weakness are involved in stochastic asset-liability modelling?
>- **Parameter sensitivity**: The results are highly sensitive to the assumed parameter values such as the mean and variance of investment returns which can result in spurious accuracy
>- **Computational constraints**: Running thousands of simulations is time consuming and expensive. Simplified versions of the model can compromise the complex real world dynamics that are being modelled.
>- **Inconsistency and static links**: If the model lacks dynamism, it may fail to reflect adjustments in valuation interest rates or management actions (like bonus declarations) in response to the simulated investment conditions which can produce very misleading results.

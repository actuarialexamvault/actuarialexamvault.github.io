> [!summary] Capital requirements for Critical Illness (CI) cover
> The following factors affect capital requirements for CI contracts:
>
> - **Contract Design** (High): Unit-linked or reviewable designs are more capital-efficient than non-linked or guaranteed products. Reviewable premiums allow the insurer to adjust for worsening experience, reducing the need for massive initial contingency loadings.
> - **Premium Frequency** (High): Regular premiums require significantly more initial capital than single premiums. Regular premium contracts often have negative initial cash flow (new business strain) because initial expenses exceed the first premium.
> - **Reserving vs. Pricing Basis** (Significant): Capital requirements increase as the difference between the pricing basis and the supervisory reserving basis grows. If the reserving basis is stronger than the pricing basis, capital must be provided to set up the required reserves at the outset.
> - **Initial Expenses** (Significant): High acquisition costs, such as commissions and complex medical underwriting, increase the initial capital strain. CI requires multi-disciplinary teams to assess medical and financial data, leading to heavy up-front expenses.
> - **Solvency Capital Requirement** (Significant): Supervisory authorities require a capital cushion to cover the unpredictability of diagnosis rates, which are less stable than mortality rates. CI involves higher prudent margins and risk-based capital to withstand extreme adverse scenarios.
> - **Guarantees** (High): Offering guaranteed premiums or benefit definitions increases the risk of needing significant reserve enhancements if experience worsens. Medical advances can lead to earlier diagnosis or windfall claims, forcing insurers to find additional capital.
> - **Late Claim Notification** (Relevant): Insurers must maintain reserves for late notified claims, which can arise up to ten years after the initial incidence. This increases the duration for which capital must be held and managed.

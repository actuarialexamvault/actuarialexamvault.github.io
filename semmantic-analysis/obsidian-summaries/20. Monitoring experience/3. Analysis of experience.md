
> [!abstract] What are the various demographic and financial investigations that an actuary might conduct?
> - Mortality (and other contingencies)
> - Persistency (withdrawal rates)
> - Expenses
> - Investment return

> [!skull] What is the process for conducting a mortality investigation?
>1. **Data collection**: a reasonable volume of stable, consistent data from the insurer's own policy records and movement data. This step includes reconciling the data against accounting records to ensure accuracy and completeness
>2. **Data grouping**: group data into homogeneous risk cells to avoid distortions cause by the change in the "mix of business"
>	- type of contract
>	- age and sex
>	- duration from entry
>	- smoker status
>	- underwriting status
>	- distribution channel
>3. **Time interval**: must be long enough for a credible amount of data to accumulate e.g. 3 to 4 years
>4. **Crude rates**: determine "crude" mortality rates for each cell
>5. **Graduation**: These crude rates are smoothed to produce a usable mortality curve
>6. **Standard table comparison**: Instead of building a new table from scratch, companies express their own experience as a percentage of a standard mortality table
>7. **A/E analysis**: The actual death strain (actual deaths multiplied by sum at risk) is compared to the expected death strain derived from the valuation or pricing basis.
>8. **Reasonability checks**: The results are checked against previous investigations or industry data
>9. **Actions**: If the analysis identifies adverse trends or significant differences from assumptions:
>	- Reprice products for new business
>	- Update valuation assumptions for supervisory reserves
>	- Modify underwriting standards
>	- Adjust (terminal) bonus rates for with-profits business to maintain equity between different ages. 

> [!lapse] What is the process for conducting a withdrawal (persistency) investigation?
> 1. **Data collection**
> 2. **Group data** into homogenous risk groups,
> 	1. Type of contract
> 	2. Duration in force
> 	3. Sales method / distribution channel
> 	4. Frequency and size of premium
> 	5. Premium collection method (cash/debit order)
> 	6. Original term of contract
> 	7. Sex and age
> 3. **Calculate persistency rates**:
> 	- *First year rates*: number of contracts that survive until their first policy anniversary divided by the number of contracts issued by the company in the the last financial year 
> 	- *Subsequent years*: a similar procedure is used, but there are two approaches:
> 		- divide by the number in force at the beginning of that year
> 		- divide by the number in force at the outset of the contract.
> 4. **Regrouping the data into broader categories** if the rates are similar at close durations or if differences are due to random fluctuations. 
> 5. **Analyse other forms of discontinuance**:
> 	1. paid-up  policies
> 	2. partial withdrawals
> 6. **Consolidate and interpret the results**: using 
> 	- the general economic environment and 
> 	- the competitive landscape or 
> 	- the perceived value of the product to the customer.

> [!receipt] What is the process for conduction an expense investigation?
> Commission is excluded from the expense investigation
> 1. **Time interval**: 12 month period
> 2. **Split expenses by function**:
> 	- Initial acquisition costs
> 	- Renewal (maintenance and administration)
> 	- Termination (maturity, surrender, or claim)
> 	- Investment costs (asset management fees)
> 3. **Determine cost drivers**: Each functional category is further analysed to determine if the expense is proportional to:
> 	- The number of contracts: per-policy costs
> 	- The amount of premium: marketing or premium collection costs
> 	- The amount of benefit (sum assured): medical underwriting or claims processing
> 4. **Allocating overhead costs**: apportioning across the functional cells:
> 	1. Salaries: Staff costs are split using timesheets for those working across multiple cells, overhead staff are allocated pragmatically, often in proportion to the direct staff costs they support
> 	2. Property costs: A notional rent is charged to each department based and floor space occupied. These costs (along with heating, lighting and taxes) and then allocated to policy cells in proportion to that departments salary costs
> 	3. Computer costs: Large capital purchases (hardware) are amortized over their useful life. Costs are then allocated by usage (e.g. time spent on quotes vs valuation runs)
> 	4. Investment costs: These are directly allocated to investment expenses and typically expressed as a reduction in yield rather than a per-policy cost
> 5. **Non-recurring exceptional items**
> 	   - **Amortisation**: Once off large costs are spread (**amortized**) over the item's future lifetime
> 	   - **Exceptiona items**: Expenses unlikely to recur are usually excluded from the analysis
> 6. **Reconciliation and validation** of total expenses against 
> 	1. management expenses as shown in the company's published accounts. 
> 	2. previous investigations (adjusted for inflation) and 
> 	3. expense ratios of competitors.

> [!trending-up] What is the process for conducting an investment return experience investigation?
> The process focuses on the actual **investment returns** achieved on the company's assets to provide feedback for the actuarial control cycle.
> - **Purpose determines valuation basis**: The choice of basis depends on the purpose of the investigation:
> 	- With-profit surplus distribution: if the goal is to determine the surplus to be distributed via the revalorisation methods, use **partially written-up book values**
> 	- For unit-linked performance: use market values
> 	- For pricing and valuation: use actual returns and compare them with the interest rate assumptions used in premium/reserving basis
> - **Subdivide the experience** by 
> 	- main asset types
> 	- fund (policyholder vs shareholder assets)
> 	- Unit-linked investment funds
> 	- return components (interest/dividend vs capital gains)
> - **Investment expenses**: the analysis should be performed both on gross and net of investment expenses (duties and commissions)
> - **Benchmarking and comparison**: common benchmarks include
> 	  - Internal assumptions used in models
> 	  - Investment indices
> 	  - Competitor performance
> - **Feeding results back into the control cycle**
> 	  - Change investment strategy to better match liabilities
> 	  - Update with profit surplus distribution strategy
> 	  - Adjust pricing for new business
> 	  - Change reserving interest rate assumptions.

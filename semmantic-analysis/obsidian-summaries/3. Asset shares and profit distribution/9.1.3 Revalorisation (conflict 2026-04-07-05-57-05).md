
> [!info] Revalorisation Method 
> Under this method, the benefits are increased each year by a proportion of the **investment income** earned on the assets backing the policies.
> 
> - **Source of Profit:** It is primarily based on the **investment income** earned on the assets backing the life fund. 💰
>     
> - **Allocation:** A high percentage (e.g., 85%–90%) of the actual investment return is legally or contractually required to be allocated to policyholders.
>     
> - **The Technical Rate:** Policies have a guaranteed minimum interest rate. The "Revalorisation" is the **excess** return above this rate. 📈
> - **Guarantees:** There is usually a minimum guaranteed interest rate (the **technical rate**). If the actual investment return is higher, the "excess" is used to provide the revalorisation. 📈If the actual investment is lower than the technical rate the insurer would have to bear the costs of the shortfall. 
> 
> More transparent but less flexible for the insurer. The insurer is fully exposed to mortality and expense overruns without being able to offset them by adjusting profit distributions.
> 

>[!note]
>[[ASSA/F102 Life Insurance Principles/Summary/3. Asset shares and profit distribution/9.1 With-profits surplus distribution|Delaying profit distribution]] is not possible in this method

> [!abstract] Comparison: Revalorisation vs. Conventional With-Profits
>| Feature | Revalorisation | Conventional With-Profits | 
>| :--- | :--- | :--- | 
>| **Discretion** | Low (Formula-driven) | High (Actuarial discretion) | 
>| **Transparency**| High (Direct link to yield) | Low (Smoothed over time) | 
>| **Smoothing** | Minimal | Significant 🌊 | 
>| **Asset Mix** | Often Fixed Interest heavy | Can be Equity/Property heavy |
>

### Derivation of the Revalorisation Rate

Let:
- $V_{t−1}$​ = The reserve (liability) at the start of the period.
- $i_t$​ = The **actual** net investment return earned by the insurer on the backing assets during period t.
- $g$ = The **technical interest rate** (the **guaranteed minimum growth** rate already priced into the contract).
- $α$ = The **percentage of profit** the insurer is contractually or legally required to share (e.g., 90%).

The "excess" investment return over the guarantee is:
$$(i_t​−g)×V_{t−1}$$​The amount of profit distributed to the policyholder ($P_t$​) is then:
$$P_t​=α×(i_t​−g)×V_{t−1}$$​To find the **revalorisation rate (rt​)** applied to the policy value, we divide the distributed profit by the opening value:

$$r_t​=\frac{P_t​}{V_{t−1}}​​=α×(i_t​−g)$$
>[!info] Distributed Profit (Pt​) 
>In many jurisdictions, if $i_t​<g$, the revalorisation rate $r_t$​ is simply 0, but the insurer must still credit the guarantee $g$. This creates the "shortfall risk", where the insurer's equity must fund the gap.

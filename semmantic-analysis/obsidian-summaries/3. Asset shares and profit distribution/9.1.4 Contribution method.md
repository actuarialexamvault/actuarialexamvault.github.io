

> [!info] The Contribution Method (USA Style) 
>The **Contribution Method** aims to distribute surplus based on exactly how much each policy "contributed" to the total surplus.
> 
> It typically breaks down the surplus into three "components":
> 
> 1. **Investment:** Surplus from actual interest earned vs. expected (technical) rate. 💰
>     
> 2. **Mortality:** Surplus from actual claims being lower than assumed in the premium. 🧬
>     
> 3. **Expenses:** Surplus from actual administration costs being lower than the expense loadings. 🏢
> 
>[[ASSA/F102 Life Insurance Principles/Summary/3. Asset shares and profit distribution/9.1 With-profits surplus distribution|Delaying profit distribution]] is still possible here, but the calculation is much more granular.

> [!abstract] 
> Under the **Contribution method**, because the formula is a multi-factor "Analysis of Surplus," the policyholder effectively shares in the insurance risk (mortality) and operational risk (expenses) alongside the investment risk. 🧬🏢

> [!math] Calculating the Mortality Profit Component
>
>The mortality surplus (S_mort​) distributed to the policyholder in year $t$ is derived as:
>
$$S_{mort}​=(q_{expected​} − q_{actual}​)×(S−V_t​)$$
>
>Where:
>- $q_{expected​}$ = The mortality rate assumed in the premium/valuation.
>- $q_{actual}$​ = The actual experienced mortality rate for that group.
>- $S$ = The Sum Assured.
>- $V_t$​ = The Reserve (or Fund Value) held for the policy.
>- $(S−V_t​)$ = The **Death Strain at Risk**. 🛡️

> [!example] The "Three-Factor" Dividend 
> The total dividend under the contribution method is the sum of the three pieces we discussed:
> 
> *Dividend = Investment Profit + Mortality Profit + Expense Profit*
> 
> Unlike the revalorisation method, if $S_{mort​}$ becomes negative (actual deaths > expected), it can swallow up the investment profit, leading to a lower total dividend for the policyholder.

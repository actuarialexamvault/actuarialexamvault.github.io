> [!summary] Overview of external environment factors
> This section describes the influence of the general business environment on the level of risk to the insurer, covering: consumer propensity, distribution channels, expenses and commission, economic environment, legal environment, fiscal regime, and professional guidance.

> [!summary] 1. Volume and mix of new business
> The insurer should consider the expected **volume of new business**, since this affects profitability, expense recovery, solvency strain and operational capacity.
>
> If volumes are too low, the insurer may not recover fixed costs such as product development, systems, marketing, distribution and regulatory approval costs.
>
> If volumes are too high, the insurer may suffer from **new business strain**, especially where initial expenses, commission or reserving requirements are high.
>
> The insurer should also consider the expected **mix of business**, for example by: age, gender, sum assured or benefit level, premium term, policy duration, distribution channel, socio-economic group, underwriting class. A change in business mix may alter the risk profile, capital requirements, claims experience, expense assumptions and profitability of the business.


> [!summary] 2. Target market and customer demand
> The insurer should consider whether there is sufficient demand for the product in the intended target market.
>
> This includes considering: customers' need for the product, affordability of premiums, financial literacy and product understanding, cultural or social attitudes towards insurance, customer trust in insurers, whether the product meets policyholder reasonable expectations.
>
> If customers do not understand the product, there is a risk of poor take-up, mis-selling, complaints, reputational damage and regulatory intervention.


> [!summary] 3. Distribution channel and distributor behaviour
> The insurer should consider how the product will be distributed and whether the distribution channel is appropriate for the target market.
>
> Distribution factors include: whether the sales force has sufficient training, whether distributors understand the product, whether customers receive adequate disclosure, whether advice is suitable, whether sales incentives encourage good customer outcomes.
>
> Commission structures may create conflicts of interest. For example, high upfront commission may encourage excessive sales, poor-quality business, policy churning or mis-selling. The insurer should monitor persistency, complaints and claims disputes by distribution channel.

> [!summary] 4. Competition
> The insurer should consider the level of competition in the market.
>
> This includes competition on: premium rates, benefit levels, underwriting terms, policy conditions, service standards, claims handling, brand strength, distribution access.
>
> Strong competition may put downward pressure on premiums and profit margins. The insurer should also consider the risk that competitors introduce similar products with better terms, causing lower sales or higher lapses.

> [!summary] 5. Economic environment
> The insurer should consider the impact of economic conditions on both policyholders and the insurer.
>
> Relevant factors include: inflation, interest rates, investment returns, unemployment, wage growth, disposable income, exchange rates, general economic growth.
>
> A poor economic environment may reduce affordability, increase lapses, reduce new business volumes or cause policyholders to choose cheaper products. Interest rates and investment returns affect pricing, reserving, capital requirements and profitability. Expense inflation may increase administration and claims management costs, particularly where premiums or charges are guaranteed.


> [!summary] 6. Legal and regulatory environment
> The insurer should consider current and future legal and regulatory requirements.
>
> These may affect: product design, policy wording, disclosure requirements, underwriting, claims handling, treating customers fairly, commission and remuneration structures, reserving, solvency capital requirements, reporting requirements.
>
> Regulatory changes may increase costs or require product redesign. There is also a risk that the insurer may need to hold additional reserves or capital if the regulator considers the product to have significant guarantees or uncertain future cashflows.

> [!summary] 7. Tax and fiscal environment
> The insurer should consider the tax treatment of: premiums, benefits, investment income, reserves, shareholder profits, commissions and expenses.
>
> Tax treatment may affect the attractiveness of the product to policyholders and the profitability of the product to the insurer.
>
> Changes in tax rules may affect demand, pricing, profitability and policyholder behaviour.

> [!summary] 8. Expenses and operational capability
> The insurer should consider whether it has the systems, staff and expertise required to administer the product.
>
> Relevant expense and operational issues include: policy administration costs, claims assessment costs, underwriting costs, IT system costs, compliance costs, commission payments, customer service costs, expense inflation.
>
> If actual expenses exceed those assumed in pricing, profitability may be reduced. This is particularly important where premiums, charges or benefits are guaranteed and cannot easily be adjusted after policy inception.

> [!summary] 9. Claims environment
> The insurer should consider the expected claims experience and whether it has sufficient expertise to manage claims.
>
> Relevant issues include: claims incidence rates, claim amounts, claim duration, claims inflation, anti-selection, moral hazard, fraud risk, clarity of claims definitions, ability to verify claims objectively.
>
> Poor claims management may lead to higher costs, disputes, delays, complaints and reputational damage.


> [!summary] 10. Policyholder behaviour
> The insurer should consider how policyholders may behave after purchasing the product.
>
> Important behaviours include: lapses, surrenders, premium holidays, policy alterations, take-up of options, anti-selection, claims behaviour.
>
> Policyholder behaviour can materially affect profitability, reserves and capital requirements. For example, if policyholders are more likely to keep policies when they are valuable and lapse when they are less valuable, this may worsen the insurer's experience.


> [!summary] 11. Reinsurance availability
> The insurer should consider whether reinsurance is available and whether it is needed.
>
> Reinsurance may help with: reducing volatility, managing large claims, reducing capital requirements, accessing specialist underwriting or claims expertise, supporting new product development.
>
> However, reinsurance introduces counterparty risk and may reduce retained profits. The cost and terms of reinsurance may also affect the viability of the product.


> [!summary] 12. Capital and solvency requirements
> The insurer should consider the capital required to write the business.
>
> Capital requirements may be affected by: guarantees, long-term liabilities, uncertainty in assumptions, investment risk, lapse risk, expense risk, mortality or morbidity risk, operational risk.
>
> If capital requirements are high, the product may need higher premiums or may not meet the insurer's target return on capital. Rapid growth may also create solvency strain.


> [!summary] 13. Reputational and conduct risk
> The insurer should consider the risk of reputational damage if the product is poorly understood, poorly sold or perceived as poor value.
>
> Conduct risks include: mis-selling, unsuitable advice, unclear policy terms, claim disputes, poor complaints handling, unfair exclusions, excessive charges.
>
> Reputational damage may reduce future sales, increase regulatory scrutiny and harm relationships with distributors and policyholders.


> [!example] Condensed exam-style answer
> The insurer should consider the expected volume and mix of new business, since low volumes may not justify fixed development and administration costs, while rapid growth may create new business strain and solvency pressure. The mix of business by age, benefit level, policy duration, distribution channel and underwriting class will affect the risk profile, expenses, reserves and capital needs.
>
> The insurer should assess customer demand and affordability in the target market. Policyholders must understand the product and its limitations, otherwise there is a risk of poor take-up, complaints, policyholder dissatisfaction and regulatory intervention.
>
> The distribution channel should be considered, including the training and incentives of distributors. Commission structures may encourage mis-selling, policy churning or poor-quality business if they reward sales volume rather than persistency or customer outcomes.
>
> The insurer should consider competition in the market, including competition on price, benefits, underwriting, claims service and distribution access. Competitive pressure may reduce margins or lead to higher lapses if better products become available.
>
> Economic conditions are relevant because inflation, interest rates, investment returns, unemployment and disposable income affect affordability, persistency, claims costs, expenses, reserves and profitability.
>
> Legal, regulatory and tax developments should also be considered. Changes in solvency requirements, disclosure rules, commission regulation, tax treatment or consumer protection laws could affect product design, demand, profitability and capital requirements.
>
> The insurer should consider expense risk and operational capability. Actual expenses, claims handling costs, system costs and compliance costs may exceed those assumed in pricing, especially where premiums or charges are guaranteed.
>
> Finally, the insurer should consider claims experience, policyholder behaviour, reinsurance availability, capital requirements and reputational risk, as all of these may affect the profitability and sustainability of the business.

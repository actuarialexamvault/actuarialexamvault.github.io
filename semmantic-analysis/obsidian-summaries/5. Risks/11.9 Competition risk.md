
> [!group] Management decisions
The need to compete in a free market, may lead the management of a life insurance company to take decisions which increase its risk profile beyond that which can be supported by the available resources. Some of the decisions they might take are:
>- reduce premium rates or charges under new business contracts
>- offer additional guarantees and options under new business contracts
>- increase bonuses under existing contracts
>- increase salaries or commissions in the respective distribution channels
>- for marketing reasons, the company may not review charges upwards by as much as it should in order to cover worsening future experience.

> [!important] Impact on Pricing and Profitability**
>
>Competitive pressure often leads insurers to **reduce premium rates or charges** for new business contracts to match or undercut rivals. In highly price-sensitive channels, such as independent intermediaries, companies might set charges that cover only the **marginal costs of a policy**, thereby failing to make an adequate contribution to the company's fixed overhead expenses. If the total contribution from all products falls below the level of these fixed overheads, the company’s overall profitability is directly threatened. Furthermore, competition may prevent an insurer from **increasing reviewable charges** on existing business even when the underlying claims or expense experience has worsened.

> [!receipt] Onerous Guarantees and Options
>
>To differentiate their products in a crowded market, companies may offer **additional guarantees and options** at little or no extra cost to the policyholder. Historically, competition has pushed some insurers into offering guarantees before the true cost of **reserving for those future risks** was fully estimated or priced. These features increase risk because the company commits to specific terms—such as guaranteed annuity rates or minimum maturity values—decades before the actual cost is known.

> [!info] Bonus and Distribution Pressures**
>
>In the with-profits market, insurers may feel compelled to **increase bonus declarations** beyond what the underlying asset shares can sustainably support to maintain a competitive position against other offices. Similarly, competition for "shelf space" within distribution channels can lead to **higher commissions for intermediaries** or increased salaries for sales forces. These higher acquisition costs increase the **initial capital strain** on the company, meaning more capital is required to write each new policy.

> [!trending-up] Compounding Effect of Business Volume**
>
>The risks associated with competitive pricing and generous guarantees are often **compounded by high volumes of new business**. While high sales are typically a goal, writing an unexpectedly large volume of capital-intensive business can **deplete an insurer’s free assets** and required solvency margins. If the company has reduced its margins to be competitive, it may find that it lacks the capital resources to support its own rapid growth, potentially leading to **supervisory insolvency**.

> [!lapse] Marketing and Reputational Risks**
>
>Failure to remain competitive can lead to a **bad reputation in the market**, which travels fast and damages future sales. However, if an insurer over-promises through competitive bonus rates and then must cut them to reflect "financial reality," it faces **marketing risk** and potential policyholder dissatisfaction. This disappointment can lead to **increased withdrawals (lapses)**, which further increases the actual average administrative cost for the remaining policies

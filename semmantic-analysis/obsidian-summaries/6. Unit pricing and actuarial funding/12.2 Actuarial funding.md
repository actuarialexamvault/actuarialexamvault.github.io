
> [!definition]
> Actuarial funding is a technique used by life insurance companies to capitalise charges above those needed for recurring expenses at inception. It reduces the total reserves held by the company thus reducing new business strain.
> It eliminates investment risk because of the mismatch between initial expenses (generally a monetary amount) and charges (% of fund over time).
> 

> [!info] Purpose and rationale
> Unit-linked contracts often incur very high initial expenses (such as commission and medical underwriting) that exceed the first premium, resulting in a large [[ASSA/F102 Life Insurance Principles/Definitions/New Business Strain#^2bbbc5|initial capital strain]]. 
> 
> The practice of funding initial expenses through **lower allocation rates** during the early policy years is no longer common and lower premium allocation rates are not consistent with [[ASSA/F102 Life Insurance Principles/Definitions/Policyholders’ Reasonable Expectations (PRE)|PRE]]. 
> 
> The expenses are usually recouped gradually via future **fund management charges**, this creates a **mismatch in timing and nature** between the immediate cash outgo and the gradual investment-linked income. 
> 
> Actuarial funding addresses this by allowing the company to hold a **smaller unit reserve at inception** than the actual value of the units purchased by the premium. The company essentially "takes credit" in advance for a portion of the future management charges to cover its up-front costs. 

> [!cycle] The mechanism - Actuarial funding factor
> Instead of being "fully funded", where assets held exactly match the bid value of allocated units, the company holds the actuarial present value of the unit fund. This approach is possible because the unit fund is a contingent liability, it is only required when events like death or survival to maturity occur.
> - Funding factor: The normal fund value is multiplied by an actuarial funding factor, typically an endowment assurance function ($A_{x+t:\overline{n-t|}}$), to determine the required reserve. So the present value of a unit-linked endowment assurance at policy duration $t$ allowing for the contingency of death would be
> $$ (UF)_t(A_{x+t:\overline{n-t|}})$$
>- Where:
>	- $(UF)_t$ is the fully funded value of the unit fund at policy duration $t$
>	- $x$ is the entry age
>	- $n$ is the policy term
>- Discount rate: The interest rate used in this calculation is usually set to less than annual fund management charge the company wishes to pre-fund. To allow for some of the FMC to cover renewal expenses. 

> [!caution] Conditions for use
> For actuarial funding to be viable and prudent, the following conditions must be met:
> - ***High management charges***: There must be a substantial level of regular management charges to "buy back" the missing units over the policy term. 
> 	- Higher management charges can be charged on all units, provided there is a sizeable quantity of FMC's to make the pre-funding worthwhile.
> 	- In the early years allocated premiums would be used to buy **capital units** which have higher charges and **accumulation units**. - this practice is no longer popular due to transparency issues.
> - ***Surrender penalties***: It is not prudent to hold a reserve lower than what would be paid if the policyholder surrendered. Therefore, the funded reserve must not be less than the surrender value (bid value minus surrender penalty)
> - ***Positive Future Cashflows***: Projections must show that future net cashflows to the insurer remain positive even after the management charges are reduced to rebuild the reserve. 
> - Contingency: The benefit must be payable in a contingent event e.g. death or sickness

> [!trending-up] Absence of investment risk
> The expected return on the unit fund is irrelevant to the discount rate because there is no investment risk in the buy-back process.
> - The liability ($l$) (the missing units) is denoted in units: $l = (n-x)* UnitPrice$
> - The source of funding (management charge) is a fixed percentage of the units. $FMC=percentage*x*UnitPrice$ 
> - Therefore: $l \approx FMC$
>
>If the unit fund doubles in value due to high investment returns, the monetary cost of buying the missing units also doubles. However, because the management charge is a percentage of that fund, the amount of money to buy those units **also doubles**. Because **both the cost of the units and the funding source (the management charges) move in direct proportion to the unit price**. The insurer effectively **"borrows" units rather than cash**, and since the "repayments" are also unit-linked, the investment risk is eliminated from the buy-back process
>


> [!EXAMPLE]
> The setup:
> - ***Single premium***: R10 000 (purchasing 10 000 units at R1 each)
> - ***Annual FMC***: 5 (levied only on units physically held)
> - ***Discount rate for funding*** $(i)$: 4% 
> - ***Policy term***: 5 years
> - **Age ($x$)**: 40 year old
> - ***Investment return $(g)$***: 7% 
> - ***Actuarial funding factor ($A_{x+t:\overline{n-t|}}$)***: A multiplier that determines the required reserve based on the 4% and the probability of the contingent events (death or maturity)
> ----
> **Year 0: Inception**
> At the start, the insurer calculates the actuarially funded unit reserve instead of holding the full R10 000.
> - ***Funding factor ($A_{40:\overline{5|}}$)***: $0.8219$ (based on ***4%*** discount over 5 years)
> - Reserve physically held: $$V_0 = R 10,000 * 0.8219 = R 8,219$$ 
> - **Cashflow to non-unit fund**: $R10,000 - R8,219$
> 	- This **positive cashflow** is used to pay for **initial expenses** like commission and underwriting. 
> - The insurer is now missing $1,781$ units that it must buy back later.
>---
**Year 1: End of the year**
> The insurer collects its charge and rebuilds the reserve toward the next required level.
> - ***Fund value***: the units the insurer physically holds have grown in value
> 	- Value of units held (after growth): $R8,219* 1.07 = R 8,794$
> - ***Inflow (FMC)***: The ***5%*** charge is levied on the value of the units actually held.   
> 	- $FMC = 8,794 * 0.05 =R440$ 
> - ***Required reserve***: The policyholder is still promised 10000 units. The full value of those units is now R10700. The insurer must now calculate and adjust the reserve at year 1 ($A_{41:\overline{4|}}=0.8548$). $$ V_1 = (10,000 * 1.07) * 0.8548 = R9,146$$
> - ***Outflow (reserve rebuild)***: The insurer must move money from the non-unit fund to "buy back" the units needed to reach the new required reserve level. $$V_1 - V_0 = R9,146 - 8,794 = R352$$
>- ***Net result for the insurer***: This profit is used to cover renewal expenses and expected death claims $$Profit = Income - Outgo = R88$$
>- The insurer is now missing $1,554$ units that it must buy back later. The number of units gradually build up to the fully funded reserve over the policy term.
>
> 	

>[!summary]
>The insurer effectively borrows units, not cash. As long as the **FMC is higher than the discount rate**, the insurer will always have enough unit-linked income to buy back the units and still retain a profit, regardless of **investment performance**.


> [!summary] Pricing
> The model is used to determine a premium, or charging structure for a new or existing product that will meet a life insurance company's **profit requirement**.
> 1. ***Model point selection***: select model points that represent the expected new business mix
> 2. ***Cashflow projection***: for each time point, the model projects 
> 	- withdrawal death and maturity benefits 
> 	- premiums, investment income, claims, expenses, tax) 
> 	- reserving and solvency requirements ((increases or releases of reserves)
> 	- Allow for interaction between variables (e.g. investment return and inflation, investment returns and bonus rates)
> 3. **Assumptions**: for expected future experience: 
> 	- mortality withdrawal and expenses 
> 4. **Consistency:** All assumptions should be consistent with each other and allow for interactions between variables (e.g. investment return and expense inflation assumption and surrender rates could be linked to the economic scenario)
> 5. **Time period**: Month/annual
> 6. The net cashflow will be the net non-unit cashflow less the increase in the non-unit reserve
> 7. ***Discounting***: the resulting net cashflows are discounted back to the inception date using a **[[ASSA/F102 Life Insurance Principles/Definitions/risk discount rate (RDR)|risk discount rate (RDR)]]**. 
> 8. ***Profit criteria***: the model produces a **[[../../Definitions/Profit signature|Profit signature]]**, which is then summarized into a single profit criterion.
>	- *NPV*: sum of discounted profit often as a percentage of discounted premiums (market share) or initial commission (effort)
>	- *IRR*: the discount rate at which the NPV is zero. This may not always exist or may be more than one solution if the net cashflows change signs more than once. 
>	- *DPP*: [[ASSA/F102 Life Insurance Principles/Definitions/Discounted Payback Period|Discounted Payback Period]], is suitable for long term contracts where the initial capital layout is large. 
> 9. ***Capital requirements***: by scaling up the model points into a full model office. The insurer can assess whether it has sufficient capital (either on a regulatory or economic capital basis) to finance the expected volumes of sales ([[ASSA/F102 Life Insurance Principles/Definitions/New Business Strain|New Business Strain]]). If not, it may reconfigure the design of the product so as to reduce the capital requirement.
> 10. ***Marketability***: if the modelled premium is too high to be competitive, the model can be used to test **changes in product features, distribution channels or profit targets** to **improve marketability and/or reduce risk** or to make a decision on whether to proceed with the product or not..
> 11. ***Sensitivity analysis***: varying parameter values (like mortality, interest rates or lapses) to see how profit is affected. This helps the actuary to assess the need for additional prudential margins in the assumptions to guard against adverse future experience.

> [!receipt] Profitability of existing business
> 12. **Data**: The assessment can be performed using:
> 	- ***Full policy data***: on a policy-by-policy basis, requires computing resources and time.
> 	- ***Model points***: group policies into homogeneous groups. Verification may be done by **calculating the reserves** on them and comparing them with the published value.
> 13. ***Cashflow projection***: the model projects all real and notional cashflows for each policy or model point. 
> 14. ***Discounting***: the net cashflows are discounted back to the valuation date to determine the PVFP (Present Value of Future Profits which feeds into [[ASSA/F102 Life Insurance Principles/Summary/9. Assumptions/Valuation assumptions/15.14 Embedded value#^00d738|Embedded Value]]).
> 15. ***Risk discount rate (RDR)***: the RDR used for existing business is typically lower than the rate used for pricing new business because the risks associated with new business (e.g. uncertainty over sales volumes and mix of business) are no longer applicable to in-force policies.
> 16. ***Use of results***: Management uses these models to gain insights such as:
> 	- *Breakdowns*: analysing by **product line, product class, distribution channel or subsidiary** to compare **relative contributions to the firm**
> 	- *Financial health check*: simply checking that the PVFP for a specific product is **positive** serves as an indicator that its nothing is badly going wrong with the financial management of that product.
> 	- *Appraisal value*: when the company is valued for sale, the **embedded value** serves as the starting point, which is then added to goodwill (the value of future new business) to determine the **appraisal value**.

> [!solvency] Assessing solvency
> This assessment involves comparing the value of the company's assets against its liabilities using either supervisory (regulatory) or economic capital requirements.
> 17. ***Static vs dynamic***: static solvency testing only measures a company's financial position at a single point in time, dynamic solvency testing uses a full model office to project the revenue account and balance sheet forward for 3 to 5 years. 
> 	- Assesses the company's **resilience** to changes in the [[ASSA/F102 Life Insurance Principles/Summary/4. External environment/10. The external environment|external economic environment]].
> 	- Identify is the company's surplus assets might be likely to run out or become negative in the near future.
> 	- Provide management with enough time to adjust strategies - such as changing investment strategy, reduce bonus distributions, or alter marketing plans - to reduce the risk of insolvency/
> 18. ***Deterministic vs stochastic***: actuaries use deterministic projections to test the impact on solvency, of specific **scenarios**, such as a gradual fall in investment returns or faster-than-expected mortality improvements on. Regulators may specify which deterministic scenarios must be tested. Stochastic projections uses *Monte Carlo* simulations to generate thousands of potential future paths for variables like in the balance sheet and revenue account. This allows the **actuary** to quantify the **probability of ruin**.
> 19. ***Modelling challenges***: When projecting solvency stochastically, a major computational challenge is **nested simulations**, where the projected balance sheet for a future year itself requires thousands of simulations to value complex features like financial guarantees or options. Closed-form approximations (such as the Black-Scholes formula) or **proxy models** can be used to estimate these values efficiently.
> 20. ***Probability of absolute ruin***: for a closed fund (no new business), an actuary may project just the **revenue account** until the very **last contract expires**. If the accumulated net cashflow up to that time is **positive**, company is solvent for that run. The proportion of simulations where the company runs out of assets is the estimated probability of absolute ruin. 
> 21. ***Solvency capital requirements (risk margin)***: Models are used to determine the level of required solvency capital, which acts as a protective cushion. Under risk-based regimes like [[Value-at-Risk (VaR)]], models subject the balance sheet to extreme "shocks" (such as a 1-in-200-year event) to ensure the company remains solvency over a defined period, usually over one year. This requires the model to account for the benefits of **diversification** within its portfolio as it is unlikely that all extreme risks will happen at the same time.

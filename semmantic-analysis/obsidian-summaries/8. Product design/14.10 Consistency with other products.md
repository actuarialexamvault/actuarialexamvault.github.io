
> [!info] Consistency
> Maintaining consistency with the existing product range is essential for both **operational efficiency and policyholder equity.**
>- **Operational Efficiency:** Similar charging and benefit structures across a product line save time and costs related to **systems development, training administrative and sales staff**, and printing marketing materials.
>
>- **Policyholder Equity:** A design that appears significantly more attractive than existing business may be perceived as **unfair by current policyholders.** This dissatisfaction can lead to a **marketing risk**, where existing clients surrender their policies in mass, preventing the company from recovering its fixed overhead costs.
>
>- **Ease of Transition:** Consistency ensures that new products align with the company's established brand and the expectations of its distribution channels.

> [!example] Interaction of product design factors
> Product design factors are **not independent**; meeting one objective frequently prejudices another, meaning the final design must be an **optimal compromise** between customer needs and insurer objectives.
> A core interaction exists between **profitability and risk control versus marketability and competitiveness**
> **Risk Management vs. Sales Volume:** While rigorous underwriting and claims management reduce the risk of adverse experience, **onerous processes can act as a barrier to sales**, negatively impacting business volumes
> **Simplicity vs. Equity (Cross-subsidies):** A simple charging structure is easier for customers to understand and for systems to handle, but it often creates **conscious cross-subsidies** where larger policies overpay to support the administration of smaller policies
> **Consistency vs. Innovation:** New designs must be **consistent with the existing product range** to ensure operational efficiency and to avoid "unfairness" that might trigger mass surrenders of older policies
> **Distribution Channels:** The intended method of sale dictates design; **direct marketing requires simple products**, whereas more complex "bells and whistles" may only be successful when explained by professional intermediaries

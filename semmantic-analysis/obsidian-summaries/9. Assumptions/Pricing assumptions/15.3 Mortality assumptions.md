
> [!skull] Base mortality
>- The initial mortality rates should reflect the expected future experience of the **target market**, **distribution channel** and the **underwriting** applied at the point of sale. 
>- ***Data sources***: Actuaries typically adjust **standard mortality table**s rather than creating new ones. If a company has **sufficient data**, it uses its own **historical experience** or the experience of a **similar class of business**. If internal data is insufficient, industry sources (such as CMI reports in the UK), population statistics, or data from reinsurers are used.
>- ***Homogenous grouping***: data must be divided into homogeneous groups, typically by **age, sex and smoker status** subject to adequate levels of data being retained within each cell to remain credible. 
>- ***Historical investigation period***: investigations usually cover a 3 to 4 year period to ensure adequate data volume without introducing excessive heterogeneity from **different generations** and trends.

> [!trending-down] Mortality trends
>Importance of modelling trends in mortality
>- ***Longevity risk***: mortality trends are are critical for contracts where longevity risk is a major factor e.g. immediate annuities
>- ***Death benefits***: while mortality improvements tend to benefit products with that pay death benefits, expectations of **increasing mortality** due to factors like pandemics or diseases (e.g. AIDS) should be included in the basis.
>
>**Approaches to determining trends**
>- ***Expectation approach***: involves using **expert opinion** and **subjective judgement** to specify future scenarios and can be subject to bias
>- ***Extrapolation approach***: projects historical trends into the future. This requires subjective judgement to determine which past periods are most relevant for the projection
>- ***Explanatory approach***: this models **bio-medical processes** for **major causes of death** such as heart disease and cancers. It is only effective to the extent that these processes are well understood
>
>**Advanced modelling** 
>- ***Cohort effect***: observed mortality improvements can vary significantly based on the year of birth. Deterministic projections need to allow for different improvement rates by year of birth.
>- ***Stochastic projections***: Models like Lee-Carter generate many different improvement rate scenarios. The models are difficult to calibrate and often require large datasets, such as national statistics, to ensure volume of data is sufficient for credible results.
>- ***Predictive modelling***: Multi-factor techniques, such as GLMs are used to combine internal customer data with external drivers while allowing for inter-correlations.
>
>Ultimately, these trends represent an "**expanding funnel of doubt**," as uncertainty increases the further into the future a parameter is projected. Therefore, pricing assumes a **margin for prudence** based on the statistical risk and the financial significance of the mortality assumption to the company

> [!In-flow] External sources of data
> ***Industry-wide mortality investigations***: useful if no standard table exists for the specific type of cover, can show trends.
> ***Population statistics***: useful for showing mortality trends
> ***Reinsurance data***: cover a wider range of lives. contract types, geographies. Relevant for new ventures, aggregated data from different companies with unique target markets and underwriting procedures, extra cost, introduces counterparty risk


> [!explainer] Using the 5-step framework for setting the investment assumption
>- **Identify importance**: because the contracts build up large reserves and involve a fixed monetary guarantee at maturity, the investment return assumption is **very important**.
>- **Asset choice**: typically government or corporate fixed interest securities will be selected to match the liabilities.
>- **Best estimate**: The actuary uses the concept of discounted mean term (duration) to select assets whose proceeds will align with the expected timing of liability outgo. The starting point is the current **weighted average redemption yield** available on matching securities in the market. 
>- **Future changes**: For a regular premium contract and/or coupon paying bonds, there is a need to account for the fact that future premiums and coupon payments will be received at various points and must be invested at unknown future interest rates. 
>- **Taxation**: if the company is taxed on investment income, it must net the investment return for taxation to reflect the actual investment return it will receive.
>- **Balancing credibility and judgement**: The actuary will have to balance the amount of weight that they place on historical experience and expected changes. The final estimate is a **weighted adjustment** of the best estimate parameter and expected changes.
>- **Margins for prudence**: to protect the company against the risk that future yields are lower than anticipated, a margin is deducted from the established expected investment return.

> [!caution] Key factors influencing the investment assumption
>- ***Significance of the assumption***:  The first step is to establish **how important** the assumption is for the results, which will depend on the **level of reserves** and  **investment guarantees given**.
>- ***Onerous guarantees***: Onerous guarantees require the insurer to be more cautious in asset selection, which should be reflected in a lower (more prudent) investment return assumption.
>- ***Reinvestment risk***: If the insurer expects positive cashflows in the future (e.g. from regular premiums or bond coupons), which must be invested at unknown future rates, it faces the risk that future yields will be lower than expected. 
>- ***Taxation***: If the insurer is subject to tax on its investment income, the actuary must **net down the interest rate** to reflect the actual investment return.
>- ***Asset mix***: the assumption should reflect the intended mix of assets (equities, bonds, property, etc.) and the current and likely future returns on those specific assets. 
>- ***Free assets***: The asset mix is also influenced by the company's free assets as higher assets will allow for a riskier, higher yielding investment strategy.
>- ***With-profit business***: Insurers may use conservative investment assumptions to implicitly allow for future bonus declarations, or use realistic assumptions with an explicit "bonus" loading.

> [!trajectories] Different valuation approaches
> The approach to setting the investment assumption varies depending on the valuation methodology used:
> - **Traditional approach**: involves a best estimate based on a risk-free rate (like government bonds) plus a margin for higher risk assets like equities.
> - **Market-consistent approach**: the expected investment return is set as the risk free rate regardless of the actual assets held. For stochastic models, additional assumptions for volatility and correlation are required, which do depend on the underlying asset types.
